Why I left Merck after seven years

Notes on choosing to leave a Fortune-100 product role to run small ventures full-time.

I spent seven years owning the product roadmap for five clinical-data systems at Merck. The work was meaningful, the people were good, the salary was real. In November 2025 I left. Here's the honest version of why.

The work was actually good

I want to start there because the easy story to tell is "I left because of X bad thing." That isn't true. The Scale IT for Clinical Portfolio program removed about ten thousand hours a year of manual effort from biostatisticians who deserved their evenings back. The A and R Submission Translation platform cut the China NDA cycle from six or seven months to about a month, which translates to faster regulatory filings and faster medicines. I mentored a Cornell Tech intern named Jenny through her summer with us and her parting note is still the line I think about more than any annual review. I had my OUTPERFORMED years and my just-fine years. I worked with people I'd happily work with again.

If the work was good, why leave?

Three things changed at the same time

The first was that my side projects stopped feeling like side projects. I'd been building free public-good sites on nights and weekends for years — KiddieWordle in seventeen languages, BestPythonCourse, the free rate-finder tools, eventually SafeInk. The hours I spent on those were the hours I felt most alive. Inside Merck I was a product manager. Outside Merck I was building things. The label that was on my paycheck wasn't the label that described what I actually did with my time anymore.

The second was my son. I have a young son. I want him to see me build things, not just hear me describe the things I built. The compromise version of "I work on cool things at a big company" doesn't translate well to a four-year-old. The version where dad's name is on the storefront, where dad teaches Python in his family's village in India, where dad's free game is what dad's son's friends play — that translates. I want the words to match the picture.

The third was that I started doing the math on what I wanted my next decade to look like. Seven more years at Merck would have been comfortable. Seven more years building things I owned, with people I chose, in a city I'd planted roots in, would be the version of comfortable I actually wanted. Both paths were available. I picked the one that scared me more.

The honest tradeoffs

I left a real salary. I left a real benefits package. I left a real product team that I respected. None of those are small things.

What I got back:

  • Hours. Whole days I used to spend in meetings or in commute now go to the brands and the writing and my son.
  • The right to say no. I no longer have to ship anything I don't think should ship.
  • A more honest relationship with my own work. When KiddieSketch makes a sale, that sale is mine. When KiddieWordle gets a thousand kids in seventeen languages, those kids are mine to think about. When SafeInk routes a source to a journalist, that's a thing I built with my hands. The blast radius is small, but the line between what I do and what comes out the other side is very short.
  • My father's promise. He always said he'd give back to the village he came from. I'm setting up a Python and generative-AI teacher position there. I needed the time and the focus to do that properly. Now I have both.

What I'm not saying

I'm not saying everyone should leave their corporate job. I'm not saying Merck did anything wrong. I'm not saying I'll never go back to corporate — if the right product role at the right mission-driven org came along, I would think hard about it. I'm not saying small business is romantic; it's mostly Klaviyo flows and Meta Ads dashboards and Slack pings from my own pipeline bot.

I'm just saying: there comes a moment where the most honest description of your week stops matching the most honest description of your title. When that happens, the title usually loses, even if the title pays more.

That moment came for me last November. I haven't looked back since.
