To the Internet Archive team,

I'm writing about the Program Coordinator role. I want to work at the Internet Archive because the work I already do on my own time points in the same direction your organization does: useful tools and knowledge should be free, reachable, and built for the people who need them most. KiddieWordle is a Wordle for younger players in seventeen languages with no ads in the play area. BestPythonCourse is a free guide for people who want to learn Python without being upsold into a $300 course. LoanRatesFinder and FreeRateFinder are free mortgage and loan-rate references with simple calculators. None of those projects will make anyone rich. That's the point — I keep building them anyway. The Internet Archive is the closest thing I've found to a job where that same instinct is the work, not the side practice.

For seven years at Merck I coordinated a portfolio of clinical-trial analysis and reporting systems used by teams in the US, Europe, and Asia-Pacific. The shape of that work translates closely to what a Program Coordinator at the Archive does day to day. I led a multi-system program called Scale IT for Clinical Portfolio that eliminated roughly ten thousand hours of manual work a year across four interconnected systems — the same pattern as freeing a small staff to do mission work instead of repetitive prep. I designed and shipped an A&R Submission Translation platform that cut a regulated submission cycle from about six months to about a week, through user research with stakeholders in Tokyo, Shanghai, Frankfurt, Brussels, and the US. I served as the regulatory-audit subject-matter expert for three systems, which is the same documentation rigor a grant report or an accession workflow requires. I was promoted to Squad Lead in 2022, owned sprint planning and delivery for a cross-functional team, and was rated OUTPERFORMED in both 2020 and 2021. A Cornell Tech master's intern I mentored that summer wrote: "I truly don't think I would have completed my internship so successfully without your mentorship." That sentence is the one I'm proudest of from the whole tenure.

I'm comfortable working across time zones and languages — I'm trilingual (English, Hindi, Gujarati), and I've shipped products with input from teams in Japan, China, Germany, Belgium, the US, and continental Europe. I'm comfortable being the central operations person on a small program, which is what these roles often actually are at non-profits. I'm comfortable with the tools I'd guess the Archive uses (JIRA, Confluence, Slack, Google Workspace, SQL, Python where useful, Salesforce-class CRMs at working depth) and comfortable picking up the ones I haven't met yet. I hold a Master's in Data Analytics from Penn State, Certified AI Product Management, Certified Agile Leader from Scrum Alliance, and Mental Health First Aid — that last one because too many people I respected were struggling silently and I wanted to be useful before they had to ask.

I'd be a candidate, not a perfect fit on paper for the specific title. My title at Merck was Product Manager, not Program Coordinator. I read that as an honest fact to put in front of you, not an apology for it. The skills underneath — multi-stakeholder coordination, documentation discipline, working in regulated and audit-sensitive contexts, mentoring — are the ones the job seems to ask for. If the role is more senior than the title suggests, even better. If it's more entry-level than I'd guess from the description, I'm also willing to have that conversation honestly. I'd rather start at the right level at an organization whose mission I'd be proud to point my son toward someday than chase a title at one I wouldn't.

My résumé, tailored to this role, is at riketpatel.com/resume/internet-archive/. My broader portfolio of side projects, including the free sites mentioned above, is at riketpatel.com. I'd welcome a conversation whenever it's useful.

Thank you for the time and the work you do.

Sincerely,
